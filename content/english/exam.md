#### 3. What is the difference between an immediate family and an extended family?

An **immediate family**, or a **nuclear family**, is the smallest family unit: parents and their children. An **extended family** is much bigger. It also includes grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins and other **relatives**, so it usually has two or three generations.

For example, my immediate family is my parents, my younger sister Anya and me. My extended family also includes my grandparents and my mum's sister, Aunt Olga, who lives in Moscow with her husband and their two children, my cousins.

In the past, extended families in Russia often lived together under one roof and helped each other with the housework and the children. Nowadays most people in cities live in nuclear families, but relatives still **support** each other. In our family, all the relatives **gather together** for big celebrations like New Year, and those are the noisiest and happiest days of the year!

### Theme 1.3

#### 12. What is Alexander Stepanovich Popov famous for?

Alexander Stepanovich Popov was a Russian physicist and electrical engineer. He is famous as one of the **inventors** of radio.

For several years Popov **conducted research** on electromagnetic waves, and on the 7th of May 1895, at a meeting of the Russian Physical and Chemical Society in St Petersburg, he demonstrated a device that could receive signals without wires. It was one of the first radio receivers in the world. Later he developed radio communication for the Russian Navy. In 1900 his radio **equipment** helped to rescue a group of fishermen who were carried out to sea on an ice floe in the Gulf of Finland. The same year his system won a Grand Gold Medal at the World Exhibition in Paris.

Popov was also a talented teacher, and in 1905 he became the first elected director of the St Petersburg Electrotechnical Institute. Russia remembers him every year on the 7th of May, Radio Day.

#### 13. What activities did Alexander Stepanovich Popov enjoy as a child, and how did they influence his future career?

As a child, Popov loved building machines, and this hobby prepared him for his future career as an **inventor**.

He grew up in Turyinskiye Rudniki, a mining village in the Urals with mines and workshops. Together with his friends he built small water mills with moving wheels on streams and little lifting machines with buckets. He often went to the mine workshops and spent hours watching the machines work. He was **fascinated** by an electric bell and a battery that he saw in the mine manager's house, and as a boy he even made an electric alarm clock. His sister's husband also taught him carpentry.

These activities developed his practical skills. Later, in his laboratory in Kronstadt, Popov made a lot of his instruments and **equipment** with his own hands. So his childhood interest in machines and electricity **turned out** to be the first step towards the invention of radio.

#### 14. What can you say about A.S. Popov's family? How did it influence his education?

Popov came from a large family that wasn't rich, and this decided where he got his first education.

His father, Stepan Petrovich, was a priest, and there were seven children in the family. Priests' sons usually studied at church schools, where education was free, so at the age of ten Alexander was sent to a theological school in Dalmatovo, then to Yekaterinburg, and later to the Perm Theological Seminary. His father hoped he would become a priest too, but at the seminary Alexander fell in love with maths and physics. The family was very close: the older children always **supported** the younger ones.

In 1877 Popov **entered** St Petersburg University. Because his father didn't have enough money, he was freed from **tuition fees** and later got a **scholarship**. He also gave private lessons and worked as an electrician to **make ends meet**.

#### 15. Why is May 7, 1895, considered the birthday of radio?

The 7th of May 1895 is considered the birthday of radio because on that day Alexander Popov first publicly demonstrated his radio receiver. In the old Russian calendar, it was the 25th of April.

Popov had **conducted research** on electromagnetic waves for several years. On that day, at a meeting of the Russian Physical and Chemical Society in St Petersburg, he gave a talk called "On the Relation of Metallic Powders to Electric Oscillations" and showed his device. This simple **equipment** could receive electromagnetic waves at a distance without any wires. Its main part was a coherer, a glass tube with metal powder. When a signal came, an electric bell rang, and its little hammer shook the tube, so the receiver was ready for the next signal. The **inventor** also used a long wire as an antenna.

In 1945, fifty years later, the 7th of May became Radio Day, and it is still celebrated in Russia.

#### 16. How did the Alexander Stepanovich Popov’s contemporaries characterize him?

People who knew Popov remembered him as a talented, hard-working and very modest man.

His colleagues said that he loved his work more than anything: day or night, holiday or workday made no difference to him. They also said he had "golden hands", because he could make almost any instrument himself. His students admired him as a teacher: he explained difficult ideas clearly and simply and showed brilliant experiments. One of them wrote that Popov answered every question so kindly and **sincerely** that students were never afraid to ask again.

People also described him as tactful, responsive, punctual and polite to everyone, whatever their position. He was a really **reliable** person. He was also **humble** about his success: his co-workers never noticed that he felt superior or tried to show how important his discoveries were. Interestingly, even as an experienced lecturer, he was nervous before every lecture, which shows how responsible he was.

#### 17. What is Andrei Nikolaevitch Tikhonov famous for?

Andrei Nikolaevitch Tikhonov was an outstanding Soviet mathematician and geophysicist. He is famous for his discoveries in many areas of mathematics and for his great role in **applied science**.

When he was still young, he made important discoveries in topology: there's a famous Tikhonov theorem, and some spaces are even called Tikhonov spaces. Later he **conducted research** in mathematical physics and geophysics, and he was one of the inventors of the magnetotelluric method for studying the Earth's crust. His best-known achievement is the theory of ill-posed problems and the Tikhonov regularization method, which is used today in medical tomography and image processing. He also took part in the Soviet atomic project.

In 1970 Tikhonov founded the Faculty of Computational Mathematics and **Cybernetics** at Moscow State University and was its dean for twenty years. He was an academician and twice a Hero of Socialist Labour.

#### 18. How did Andrei Nikolaevitch Tikhonov’s early life and family background influence his education and career?

Tikhonov grew up in a family that valued education, and this gave him an excellent start. At the same time, difficult years taught him to work hard from an early age.

He was born in 1906 in the town of Gzhatsk, which is now called Gagarin. His father, Nikolai Vasilievich, was a merchant, and his mother, Mariya Nikolaevna, was a warm-hearted, capable and energetic woman who took care of their two sons. The family was well-off, and the boys got the best education possible. In 1910 the Tikhonovs moved to Moscow, where the brothers went to a gymnasium.

After the revolution life became hard, and at the age of thirteen Andrei had to work as an office clerk while continuing his studies. Still, he was so talented that at fifteen he **entered** Moscow University to study mathematics. So his family's **support** and his early habit of hard work helped him **master** mathematics very quickly.

#### 19. Why was the publication of A.N. Tikhonov's first paper significant?

The publication of Tikhonov's first paper was significant because it showed his outstanding talent very early and started his brilliant career in science.

The paper was published in 1925, when Tikhonov was only nineteen and still in the middle of his **undergraduate course**. It was connected with the results of two famous topologists, Pavel Aleksandrov and Pavel Urysohn, about the conditions under which a topological space is metrisable. In simple words, that's when we can measure distances in it. It's very unusual for a student to publish serious research at that age.

Aleksandrov later said that Tikhonov began his scientific career brilliantly at nineteen with several remarkable topological results, which soon made him world-famous. One of these early ideas led to the Tikhonov theorem, which is still one of the most cited results in topology. So this first paper **turned out** to be the start of a great **researcher's** path.

#### 20. What qualities of Andrei Nikolaevitch Tikhonov's personality are highlighted in the text?

The text shows Tikhonov not only as a great scientist but also as a very warm and pleasant person.

First of all, he was a **fascinating** personality. He had an impressive appearance, he was always elegantly dressed, and he spoke in a cheerful and positive way. He was a really **upbeat** man with a big smile. He also had natural wit, wisdom, humanity, self-irony and a great sense of humour, so he could even laugh at himself.

At the same time, he had a great sense of responsibility. He was serious and very demanding about science, but he treated his colleagues and students with warm-heartedness and good will. He always **supported** young scientists, so people called him a wonderful mentor and a role model.

Finally, the text shows that he was very talented and devoted to science, with an unusually wide range of interests, from topology to geophysics.

**Useful phrases:** *a sense of humour*, *warm-hearted*, *a role model*

#### 21. Why did Andrei Nikolaevitch Tikhonov's colleagues consider it a privilege to work with him?

Tikhonov's colleagues considered it a privilege to work with him because he was both a brilliant scientist and a caring leader.

First, he combined a great sense of responsibility with warm-heartedness towards all his colleagues and students. He was known around the world as a role model and a wonderful mentor. He helped hundreds of students: he prepared letters for them and let them **conduct research** from an early stage, so young people grew very fast next to him.

Second, he valued arguments, not ranks. He used to say: "If you have substantial arguments, just come out to the blackboard with chalk in your hand and show them."

Finally, his seriousness, good will and strict **requirements** created a very special atmosphere at the institute, where everyone worked to the highest standards. No wonder many of his students, like Alexander Samarskii, became famous **researchers** themselves.

## Unit 2. Higher Education in Russia and Abroad

### Theme 2.1

#### 22. Why do students from all over the world choose to study at British universities?

Students from all over the world choose British universities because they offer high-quality education, famous names and degrees that are respected everywhere.

First, many British universities have a long history and high places in world rankings. Oxford and Cambridge, for example, are among the best universities in the world. Second, students study in English, the language of international science and business, so they **improve** their English every day. Third, courses are relatively short: a **Bachelor's degree** usually takes three years in England, and a **Master's degree** often takes just one year. This saves time and money, although **tuition fees** for international students are quite high.

Students also like the multicultural atmosphere, strong research and good support for international students. Finally, a British degree is a big advantage on the international **job market**, and many graduates can stay and work in the UK for some time after graduation.

#### 23. What are the two most well-known universities in the UK, and what are they collectively referred to as? Why are they famous?

The two most famous universities in the UK are the University of Oxford and the University of Cambridge. Together they are often called "Oxbridge".

They are famous for several reasons. First, they are very old. Teaching at Oxford existed as early as 1096, so it's the oldest university in the English-speaking world, and Cambridge was founded in 1209 by scholars who left Oxford. Second, they are always near the top of world rankings, and **admission** is extremely **competitive**. Third, both are collegiate universities, and students study in very small groups with a **tutor**.

Finally, they have educated many great people: scientists like Isaac Newton, Charles Darwin and Stephen Hawking, many British prime ministers and lots of Nobel Prize winners. By the way, the two universities are old rivals. Every spring their teams compete in the famous Boat Race on the River Thames.

#### 24. When do students enter university in the UK, and how long does it typically take to complete a bachelor's degree?

In the UK, students usually **enter university** at eighteen, after they finish **secondary education** and pass their A-levels. A **Bachelor's degree** normally takes three years of **full-time study** in England, Wales and Northern Ireland.

At sixteen, British students take GCSE exams. Then those who want to go to university stay at school or go to a sixth-form college for two more years and study three or four A-level subjects. Their A-level results decide which university they can enter. The academic year starts in September or October.

In Scotland the system is a bit different: many students start at seventeen after their Highers, and a Bachelor's degree with honours usually takes four years. Some courses are longer too. Sandwich courses with a year in industry and integrated Master's degrees in engineering take four years, and medicine takes five or six.

**См. также:** лекция [«Vocabulary: Higher Education»](01-higher-education.md).

#### 25. What types of courses are offered by further education (FE) colleges in the UK, and who typically attends these colleges?

Further education colleges, or **FE colleges**, offer education for people over sixteen, mostly below university level. Most of their courses are practical and connected with a particular job.

FE colleges provide **vocational training** in areas like engineering, construction, IT, hairdressing, catering and health care, for example BTECs, T Levels and NVQs. Many of them also run **apprenticeships**, where students work for an employer and study at the same time. Besides, you can take A-levels there, retake your GCSEs, learn English as a foreign language or even study for a foundation degree or a Higher National Diploma.

These colleges are attended by very different people: sixteen- to eighteen-year-olds who have just left school, adults who want to change their career or **improve** their skills, and people who work and study **part-time**. So FE colleges are a flexible and practical way to get a good job.

**См. также:** лекция [«Vocabulary: Higher Education»](01-higher-education.md).

#### 26. How do students apply for a place at a UK university, and what information do they need to provide?

Students apply to UK universities online through a central service called UCAS, the Universities and Colleges Admissions Service. With one application, an **applicant** can choose up to five courses.

When they **complete** the online form, students give their personal details, their education history, the qualifications they already have and their predicted A-level grades. They also write a personal statement. Now it consists of three short answers: why they want to study the course, how their studies have prepared them, and what else they have done outside school. A teacher writes a reference, and the applicant pays a small fee.

The main deadline is in the middle of January, but for Oxford, Cambridge and most medical courses it's the 15th of October. Some courses also have **admission** tests and interviews. Then universities send offers, which are usually conditional: the student must get certain grades in the **final exams**.

#### 27. What is a conversion course in the context of UK higher education, and what purpose does it serve?

A **conversion course** is a postgraduate course for people who already have a degree but want to change their field and start a new career. It "converts" your first degree into a qualification in a different subject.

For example, a history **graduate** who wants to become a lawyer can take a law conversion course instead of doing a second Bachelor's degree. There are also popular conversion courses in computer science, data science and psychology. They usually last one year of **full-time study** or two years part-time, and many of them are Master's courses.

The main purpose is to help people retrain quickly. Employers need more specialists in areas like IT, so conversion courses **meet the needs** of the **job market**. They also give graduates a second chance if they realise that their first subject wasn't the right choice.

**Useful phrases:** *to change your field*, *to retrain*, *instead of doing*, *a second chance*

**См. также:** лекция [«Vocabulary: Higher Education»](01-higher-education.md).

#### 28. What is a sandwich course in the context of UK higher education, and how can it help students after graduation?

A **sandwich course** is a degree course that includes a period of work in a real company. Study and work are layered, like bread and filling in a sandwich.

The most common type lasts four years instead of three: students study at university for two years, then spend a year in industry, which is called a placement year, and come back for their final year. There are also "thin" sandwich courses, where several shorter work placements alternate with study. Placements are often paid.

Such courses help students a lot after graduation. They get **hands-on experience** and understand how theory works in practice. They make useful contacts, their CV looks much stronger, and they can choose their future **occupation** more confidently. Many students even get a job offer from their placement company. That's why **graduates** of sandwich courses usually find jobs faster on the **job market**.

#### 29. What options are available to students after earning their undergraduate degree in the UK?

After getting an undergraduate degree in the UK, students have several options: they can start working, continue their studies or take a break to get some experience.

Many **graduates** look for a job straight away, often through graduate schemes, which are training programmes at big companies. Others choose postgraduate study. They can take a taught Master's, such as an MA or an MSc, which usually lasts one year, or a research degree like an MPhil. The next step is a **Doctorate (PhD)**, which normally takes three or four years: students **conduct research** and write a thesis. There are also postgraduate diplomas and certificates, for example the PGCE for future teachers, and **conversion courses** for people who want to change their field.

Some graduates prefer to do an **internship**, volunteer, travel during a gap year or even start their own business.

#### 30. Oxford and Cambridge are collegiate universities. What does that mean? (according to the video in ex. 18)

A collegiate university is a university made up of many independent colleges. Oxford has thirty-nine colleges and four permanent private halls, and Cambridge has thirty-one colleges.

Every student is a member of two institutions at the same time: the university and a college. The university is responsible for the academic side. Its departments decide the **curriculum**, organise lectures, laboratory work and exams, and finally **award degrees**. The colleges are self-governing communities with their own buildings, dining halls, libraries and gardens, and each college has its own **tutors**, students and traditions.

Students apply to a particular college, or let the university choose one for them, and the college becomes their "home" for the whole course. That's why, when an Oxford student says "I'm at Balliol", they mean their college.

So at Oxbridge you get the resources of a big university and the friendly atmosphere of a small community.

#### 31. What is the role of a college in terms of studying at Oxford or Cambridge? (according to the video in ex. 18)

At Oxford and Cambridge, a college is much more than a place to sleep: it's the centre of a student's academic and social life.

First, the college **provides students with accommodation**. Most first-year students live in college rooms, and many stay there during the whole course. Every college has a dining hall, a library, common rooms and often sports grounds. Second, colleges are responsible for **admissions**: they choose their students and hold the interviews. Third, and very importantly, they organise small-group teaching, called tutorials at Oxford and supervisions at Cambridge, while lectures and exams are organised by the university. Colleges also look after students' welfare, so if you have a problem, there is always someone to **support** you.

Finally, colleges have their own clubs, sports teams and traditions, like formal dinners in gowns. Your college is your community, and many graduates stay connected with it all their lives.

#### 32. What is the difference between "supervisions" at Cambridge and "tutorials" at Oxford? (according to the video in ex. 18)

In fact, there is almost no difference: "supervisions" at Cambridge and "tutorials" at Oxford are two names for the same way of teaching in very small groups.

In both universities students still **attend lectures**, but the most important learning happens in these small meetings. One, two or three students meet an expert in their subject, usually every week, for about an hour. Before the meeting they write an essay or solve a problem sheet. Then they discuss their work, defend their ideas and get detailed feedback on their **academic performance**.

The differences are only in small details. Oxford tutorials are sometimes one-to-one, while Cambridge supervisions usually have two or three students. And at Cambridge the word "**tutor**" has another meaning: a tutor there looks after students' welfare, not their studies. That's why the teacher is called a supervisor.

> [!TIP]
> Easy to remember: an Oxford tutorial = a Cambridge supervision.

#### 33. What are the two main undergraduate degree options in engineering at Oxford?

At Oxford, undergraduate engineering is one four-year course, the MEng in Engineering Science, but inside it students can choose between two main routes.

The first route is general engineering. In the first two years all students study the **core** subjects that every engineer needs, such as maths, mechanics, electronics and materials. In the third and fourth years they specialise, for example in biomedical, chemical, civil, electrical, information or mechanical engineering.

The second route combines engineering with business. At the end of the second year students can apply for the Engineering, Entrepreneurship and Management pathway, which is taught together with Oxford's business school. These students can even do an industrial placement of about six months to get **hands-on experience**. In the past there was a separate degree with a similar name, Engineering, Economics and Management.

Both routes lead to the same degree, a Master of Engineering, which is **awarded** after four years of **full-time study**.

### 57. Where is the UK located? What parts (countries) does the UK consist of?

The United Kingdom is located in the north-west of Europe, on the British Isles. Its full name is the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland, and it **consists of** four countries: England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

### 58. What is the difference between the UK and Great Britain?

The main difference is that Great Britain is a geographical name, while the United Kingdom is a political one.

Great Britain is the largest island of the British Isles, and there are three countries on it: England, Scotland and Wales. The United Kingdom **consists of** Great Britain and Northern Ireland, which is situated on another island — Ireland. So Northern Ireland is part of the UK, but not part of Great Britain. The names also come from history. The Kingdom of Great Britain appeared in 1707, when England and Scotland united. The present name has been used since 1927, after the southern part of Ireland **gained independence**. People often say "Britain" when they mean the UK, but you shouldn't call the whole country "England".

### 59. What do you know about the division of the island of Ireland into two distinct territories?

Today the island of Ireland is divided into two parts: Northern Ireland, which belongs to the United Kingdom, and the Republic of Ireland, an independent state. Dublin is **the capital of** the Republic, and Belfast is the capital of Northern Ireland.

From 1801 the whole island was part of the UK, but many Irish people wanted self-government. After the Easter Rising of 1916 and the War of Independence, the island was divided in 1921. The south became the Irish Free State in 1922 and later **gained** full **independence** as a republic in 1949. Northern Ireland **consists of** six counties in the north-east, where most people were Protestants who wanted to stay in the UK.

Later, the conflict between unionists and nationalists, who were mostly Catholic and wanted a united Ireland, led to "the Troubles" — thirty years of violence. It ended with the Good Friday Agreement of 1998, which people in both parts **voted** for. Today the border is open.

### 60. Talk about the geography and climate of the UK.

The UK is an island country in north-western Europe with an area of about 243,000 square kilometres. Its landscape is very varied, and its climate is **temperate** and **mild**, but very changeable.

In the north and west there are highlands and mountains: the Scottish Highlands with Ben Nevis, the highest mountain in the UK, the mountains of Wales and the Pennines, "the backbone of England". The south and east are mostly lowlands with green hills and fertile **plains**. The longest rivers are the Severn and the Thames. The **coastline** is long and indented, so no place in the UK is more than about 120 kilometres from the sea.

The climate is **maritime**: winters are mild, summers are cool, and it can rain in any season, especially in the west. The weather changes quickly, so it's not surprising that the British talk about it so much!

### 61. Talk about the geographical location of the UK. Where is the country located? What water bodies is the country washed by?

The UK is situated in the north-west of Europe, on the British Isles, which lie off the north-western coast of the continent. It has a **maritime location**, so the sea has always played a huge role in its history, trade and climate.

The country **is washed by** the Atlantic Ocean in the north and west and by the North Sea in the east. In the south, the English Channel separates Great Britain from France. ### 62. What type of climate does the UK have? Why?

The UK has a **temperate** maritime climate. It is **mild** and humid: winters are not very cold, summers are not very hot, and it can rain at any time of the year.

There are several reasons for this. First, the UK is an island, and because of its **maritime location** the sea keeps the air mild: water warms up and cools down slowly. Second, the warm Gulf Stream, or the North Atlantic Current, flows along the British **coastline**. Third, the winds usually blow from the south-west, from the Atlantic, and bring warm, wet air and clouds. The mountainous **terrain** in the west stops this air, so the west gets much more rain than the east.

For example, Edinburgh is almost at the same latitude as Moscow, but its winters are much warmer. The weather is also very changeable, so British people often carry an umbrella.

### 63. What were the major invasions of the British Isles? Talk about the invaders who came to the territory of the British Isles.

The British Isles were invaded many times, and every **invasion** influenced the language and culture of the country. The main invaders were the Celts, the Romans, the Anglo-Saxons, the Vikings and the Normans.

The **first inhabitants** were Stone Age people, who started building Stonehenge about 5,000 years ago. In the first millennium BC Celtic tribes came from the continent, and one of them, the Britons, gave the island its name. In AD 43 the Romans began their **conquest** of Britain. They stayed for almost 400 years and built roads, towns such as Londinium, now London, and Hadrian's Wall.

In the 5th century Germanic tribes — the Angles, Saxons and Jutes — arrived. England got its name from the Angles, and their language became Old English. From the end of the 8th century the Vikings from Scandinavia attacked the **coastline** and settled in the north and east. Finally, in 1066 the Normans conquered England — it was the last successful invasion.

### 64. Why is the year 1066 important in the history of Great Britain?

The year 1066 is one of the most important dates in British history, because it was the year of the Norman **Conquest** — the last successful **invasion** of England.

In January 1066 King Edward the Confessor died without children who could **inherit** the throne. Harold Godwinson became king, but William, Duke of Normandy, believed that the crown had been promised to him. Harold first defeated a Norwegian army in the north and then marched south to meet William. On 14 October, at the Battle of Hastings, Harold was killed, and William the Conqueror was crowned in Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day.

The results were enormous. The Normans became the new ruling class and built castles, such as the Tower of London. In 1086 the Domesday Book, a survey of land and property, was completed. For about 300 years French was the language of the court and the law, so English got thousands of French words, like "government" and "justice".

### 65. What is Danelaw?

The Danelaw was the part of England in the north and east where the Danes, or Vikings, settled and where Danish laws and customs were used in the 9th and 10th centuries.

After the Viking **invasion** of 865, the Danes began the **conquest** of the English kingdoms, and soon only Wessex was still independent. Its king, Alfred the Great, defeated them in 878 and later made a treaty with the Danish leader Guthrum. The border ran roughly along the old Roman road Watling Street, from London to Chester, and the land to the north-east of it became the Danelaw. It **consisted of** the Viking kingdom of York, East Anglia and the East Midlands.

In the 10th century Alfred's successors won these lands back, but the Scandinavian influence remained. Many place names end in "-by", like Derby and Grimsby, and English borrowed words such as "sky", "egg", "window" and even "law".

### 66. What do you know about the unification of Scotland and England?

England and Scotland were separate kingdoms for centuries and often fought each other. They were united in two steps: first under one monarch in 1603 and then into one state in 1707.

In 1603 Queen Elizabeth I of England died without children. Her relative, King James VI of Scotland, **inherited** the English throne and became James I of England. This was the Union of the Crowns: the two countries had one king, but they kept separate parliaments, laws and churches.

In 1707 both parliaments passed the Acts of Union, and the Kingdom of Great Britain was created with one Parliament in London. Scotland had economic problems, and England wanted a safe northern border. However, Scotland kept its own legal system, church and education.

Since 1999 Scotland has had its own Parliament again. Some Scots want Scotland to **gain independence**, but in the 2014 referendum about 55 percent **voted** to stay in the UK.

### 67. What do you know about the UK’s economy?

The UK has one of the largest economies in the world — it is usually in the top ten by GDP. It is a highly developed market economy based mainly on services.

The service sector produces about four-fifths of the country's wealth. Finance, banking and insurance are especially important, and London is one of the world's leading financial centres.

In the 19th century Britain was called "the workshop of the world". Today industry is smaller, but the UK still produces cars, aircraft engines, medicines and chemicals. Coal **mining** has almost disappeared, but oil and gas are still extracted in the North Sea, and the country is a world leader in offshore wind energy. Agriculture is small but efficient: farmers produce grain, meat and **dairy products**. Still, the UK has to import a lot of food and **raw materials**.

The currency is the pound sterling. The UK left the European Union in 2020.

### 68. Which sectors of the economy contribute most to the economic welfare of the UK? What industrial products does the country produce?

The service sector contributes most to the UK's economic welfare: it produces about 80 percent of GDP and employs most working people. Industry is smaller, but British factories make many high-tech products.

The leading services are finance and business services — the City of London is famous all over the world. Retail, health care, education, tourism and the creative industries are also important.

As for industry, the UK produces:
- cars, for example Land Rovers and Minis;
- aircraft engines (Rolls-Royce) and wings for Airbus planes;
- medicines and chemicals;
- electronics, machinery and military equipment;
- food and drinks, especially Scotch whisky.

The energy sector matters too: **petroleum** and natural gas come from the North Sea, and more and more electricity comes from wind farms. Agriculture gives less than one percent of GDP, but farmers produce wheat, barley, meat and **dairy products**. The UK also imports many **raw materials**.

### 69. Why do they say that the British political system is confusing?

People say that the British political system is confusing because it developed gradually over centuries, and many things work differently from how they look on paper.

Firstly, the UK has no single written constitution. The rules are found in different laws, court decisions and unwritten traditions called conventions.

Secondly, the monarch is the head of state and formally has great powers: the King opens Parliament, **appoints** the Prime Minister and gives Royal Assent to every **bill**. But in reality he "reigns but does not rule" — the real power belongs to the Government and Parliament.

Thirdly, the branches of power are not strictly separated: the Prime Minister and most ministers sit in **the House of Commons**. Until 2009 the highest court was part of **the House of Lords**, whose members are still not elected.

Finally, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own parliaments or assemblies, but England doesn't. No wonder even the British sometimes find it confusing!

### 70. What is the role of the monarch in the United Kingdom nowadays?

Today the monarch is the head of state in the UK, but his role is mostly ceremonial and symbolic. The UK is a constitutional monarchy, so the King "reigns but does not rule": real decisions are made by the Government and Parliament.

The present monarch is King Charles III. He **inherited** the throne in September 2022 after the death of Queen Elizabeth II and was crowned in May 2023.

Formally, the King has important duties. He opens Parliament and reads the King's Speech, written by the Government. He **appoints** the Prime Minister — the leader of the party with a majority in **the House of Commons** — and gives Royal Assent to every **bill**. As Walter Bagehot wrote, the monarch has "the right to be consulted, the right to encourage and the right to warn".

The King stays politically neutral. He is a symbol of national unity and the head of the Commonwealth, and he represents the country abroad.

### 71. What kind of political system does the UK have?

The UK is a constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary democracy. The head of state is a monarch, King Charles III, but the country is governed by a Government that is responsible to an elected Parliament.

The monarch **inherits** the throne, and his powers are limited by laws and traditions. Parliament is a **bicameral legislature**: it consists of **the House of Commons**, whose members are elected, and **the House of Lords**, whose members are mostly appointed. After general **elections**, which take place at least every five years, the leader of the party with a majority in the Commons becomes Prime Minister and **heads the government**.

The system has some special features. The constitution is uncodified, which means it is not written in one document. The three branches of power are closely connected. Traditionally, two parties dominate — the Conservatives and Labour. Finally, the UK is a unitary state, but Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own devolved governments.

### 72. What is parliamentary democracy? What are the main peculiarities of this political system under a constitutional monarchy?

Parliamentary democracy is a system in which the government is formed from an elected parliament and is responsible to it: it stays in power only while most MPs support it.

In the UK, people **vote** at general **elections** for members of **the House of Commons**. The party that wins most seats forms the Government, and its leader becomes Prime Minister. Ministers are also members of Parliament, so the legislative and executive powers are closely connected. If the Commons passes a vote of no confidence, the Government must resign, or new elections are held. The second-largest party forms the official Opposition, which criticises the Government.

Under a constitutional monarchy, there are two different leaders. The monarch is the head of state: he **inherits** the throne, is politically neutral and performs ceremonial duties. The Prime Minister is the head of government and has real power. So the King reigns, but the Government rules.

### 73. What are the three branches of power in the UK? Talk about each of them.

As in most democracies, power in the UK is divided into three branches: **legislative, executive and judicial**. However, in Britain they are not strictly separated, especially the first two.

1. The legislative branch is Parliament: the monarch, the House of Commons and the House of Lords. Parliament makes laws, approves taxes and controls the Government. The elected House of Commons is the most powerful part.
2. The executive branch is the Government, headed by the Prime Minister. The Prime Minister chooses about twenty senior ministers for the Cabinet, and the King formally **appoints** them. The Government runs the country and carries out **domestic and foreign policy**. Ministers are also members of Parliament.
3. The judicial branch consists of independent courts and judges. The highest court is **the Supreme Court**, created in 2009. Unlike the US Supreme Court, it cannot cancel Acts of Parliament, because Parliament is sovereign.

### 74. What chambers does the British Parliament consist of? What functions do they perform?

The British Parliament is a **bicameral legislature**: it consists of **the House of Commons** and **the House of Lords**. Formally, the monarch is also part of Parliament.

The House of Commons is the lower but much more powerful chamber. Its 650 members, called MPs, are elected by the people, one for each constituency. The Commons passes laws, approves taxes and controls the Government: ministers must answer MPs' questions. The Prime Minister and most ministers sit here, and the Speaker chairs the debates.

The House of Lords is the upper chamber, and its members are not elected. Most of them are life peers, **appointed** by the King on the advice of the Prime Minister, and there are also 26 bishops of the Church of England. Since 2026 no one can **inherit** a seat there. The Lords check and improve **bills** and suggest changes. They can delay a bill for about a year, but usually cannot stop it.

### 75. What is an uncodified constitution? What arguments do the supporters of the uncodified constitution give in favour of it?

An uncodified constitution is a constitution that is not collected in one single document — like the UK's. People sometimes call it "unwritten", but most of its rules are written — just in many different places.

Its rules come from several sources:
- important documents and Acts of Parliament, such as Magna Carta (1215), the Bill of Rights (1689) and the Human Rights Act (1998);
- common law — decisions made by judges;
- conventions — unwritten rules, for example, that the King always gives Royal Assent to a **bill** passed by Parliament.

Supporters of this system give several arguments. First, it is flexible: Parliament can change it by an ordinary law. Second, it has worked for centuries and gives stability. Third, it is democratic, because the final power belongs to the Parliament chosen at **elections**, not to judges — even **the Supreme Court** cannot cancel an Act of Parliament. Finally, it lets a strong government **make laws** and act quickly in a crisis.

### 76. Talk about the geographical location and the diversity of landscapes in the USA.

The USA is situated in the central part of North America, between the Atlantic Ocean and **the Pacific Ocean**. It is one of the largest countries in the world, and its landscapes are extremely varied — from high mountains and deep canyons to endless **plains** and hot **deserts**.

In the east there are the old, rather low Appalachian Mountains and a wide coastal plain. The centre is occupied by the Great Plains and the huge **valley** of the Mississippi, which together with the Missouri forms one of the longest river systems in the world. In the west rise the Rocky Mountains, and further west there are plateaus, the Grand Canyon and deserts with Death Valley. In the north lie the Great Lakes and Niagara Falls.

Alaska has glaciers, tundra and Denali, the highest mountain in North America, while Hawaii consists of volcanic islands. So almost every type of **terrain** can be found in the USA.

### 77. What do you know about the geographical location of the USA?

The USA is located in North America, in the Western and Northern Hemispheres, and stretches from the Atlantic Ocean to **the Pacific Ocean**.

The country consists of 50 states and the District of Columbia, where **the capital of** the USA, Washington, is situated. Forty-eight states form one territory, while Alaska lies in the north-west of the continent and Hawaii is a group of islands in the Pacific.

The USA **borders on** Canada in the north and Mexico in the south; the border with Canada is the longest land border in the world. The country **is washed by** the Atlantic in the east, the Pacific in the west, the Arctic Ocean near Alaska and the Gulf of Mexico in the south (since 2025 the US government has officially called it the Gulf of America). In the north-west, the narrow Bering Strait separates Alaska from Russia.

The USA covers about 9.8 million square kilometres and has six **time zones**.

### 78. Talk about the climatic diversity of the USA.

The USA has almost every type of climate, from Arctic in Alaska to tropical in Hawaii. The reasons are the country's huge size, its position between two oceans and its varied relief with high mountains, wide **plains** and **deserts**.

Most of the country has a **temperate** climate, but it differs a lot from region to region. The north-east and the Midwest have cold, snowy winters and warm summers. The south-east, for example Florida, is hot and humid, with a subtropical climate. California has a Mediterranean climate with dry summers and **mild** winters. The Pacific north-west, around Seattle, is cool and rainy, while the south-west is dry and very hot. Officially, the world's highest air temperature, about 57 degrees Celsius, was recorded in Death **Valley** in 1913.

The USA also has a lot of extreme weather: hurricanes on the Atlantic and Gulf coasts, tornadoes on the Great Plains, blizzards, droughts and wildfires.

### 79. Talk about the diverse population of the USA.

The USA has a very **diverse population** of about 340 million people — the third largest in the world after India and China. It is often called "a nation of immigrants", because people from almost every country have come there.

The **first inhabitants** were **indigenous peoples** — Native Americans, Alaska Natives and Native Hawaiians. Later came Europeans — the Spanish, English, Irish, Germans and many others. Many Africans were brought by force as slaves, and slavery was abolished only in 1865; today about 13 percent of Americans are African Americans. The largest minority group is Hispanics, or Latinos, mostly of Mexican origin — about one in five Americans. There are also many Asian Americans. Every year hundreds of thousands of immigrants receive American **citizenship**.

That's why America is called a "melting pot" of cultures. English is the main language, but more than 40 million people speak Spanish at home. The traditional motto of the USA is "E pluribus unum" — "Out of many, one".

### 80. What are the main sectors of the USA’s economy? How does the country influence global economy?

The USA has the largest economy in the world: it produces about a quarter of the world's GDP, roughly 30 trillion US dollars a year. It is a market economy based mainly on services.

The service sector produces about 80 percent of GDP. It includes finance, health care, trade, real estate, education, tourism, entertainment and information technology. Industry makes aircraft, cars, computer chips, chemicals and medicines, and the USA is the world's largest producer of **petroleum** and natural gas. Agriculture is highly mechanised: farmers grow corn, soybeans and wheat and produce meat, **poultry** and **dairy products**.

The USA has a huge influence on the global economy. The US dollar is the main world currency: it is widely used in international trade and makes up more than half of the world's currency reserves. American companies like Apple, Microsoft, Google and Amazon work all over the world, and decisions of the US Federal Reserve affect markets in many countries.

### 81. What is the difference between the federal government and state governments in terms of their functions? (the USA)

The USA is a federation, so power is divided between the federal government in Washington and the governments of the 50 states. The federal government deals with matters that concern the whole country, while state governments deal with matters inside their own state.

According to the Constitution, the federal government is responsible for:
- **foreign policy**, treaties and declaring war;
- the army and national defence;
- printing money;
- trade between states and with other countries;
- immigration and **citizenship**.

State governments are responsible for:
- schools and universities;
- the police and most criminal laws;
- marriage, driving licences and local roads;
- organising **elections** and local government.

Some powers are shared: both levels can **collect taxes**, borrow money, make laws and have their own courts. The Tenth Amendment says that powers not given to the federal government belong to the states or to the people. But if a state law contradicts a federal law, the federal law wins.

### 82. Talk about the executive branch of power in the USA.

The **executive branch** in the USA is headed by the President, who is both the head of state and the head of the government. This branch carries out the laws passed by Congress.

The President is elected for a four-year **term** and can serve only two terms. Americans **vote** in November, but formally the President is chosen by the Electoral College. Since January 2025 the President has been Donald Trump; it is his second term.

The President is the Commander-in-Chief of the armed forces and is responsible for **foreign policy**. He signs **bills** or vetoes them and **appoints** federal judges, ambassadors and members of the Cabinet, with the approval of the Senate. The Cabinet includes the Vice President and the heads of executive departments, such as the Secretary of State. If the President dies or resigns, the Vice President takes his place. The President lives and works in the White House in Washington, D.C.

### 83. What do you know about the two major political parties in the USA?

The USA has a two-party system: the two major parties are the Democratic Party and the Republican Party. Other parties exist, but they almost never win national **elections**.

The Democratic Party was founded in 1828. Its symbol is the donkey, and its colour is blue. Democrats are usually described as liberal or centre-left: in **domestic policy** they support a bigger role for the government in health care, education and social programmes. Well-known Democratic presidents are John F. Kennedy and Barack Obama.

The Republican Party, also called the GOP, or "Grand Old Party", was founded in 1854 by opponents of slavery, and its first president was Abraham Lincoln. Its symbol is the elephant, and its colour is red. Republicans are usually described as conservative or centre-right: they prefer lower taxes, a smaller federal government and traditional values. Ronald Reagan and Donald Trump are well-known Republicans.

Every four years Americans **vote** for a President from one of these parties.

### 84. Talk about the legislative branch of power in the USA and specify its main functions.

The legislative branch of the USA is Congress. It is a **bicameral legislature** that consists of two houses — **the Senate** and **the House of Representatives**. Congress meets in the Capitol in Washington, D.C.

The main function of Congress is to make laws. A **bill** must be passed by both houses in the same form and then signed by the President. If the President vetoes it, Congress can still pass it by a two-thirds majority in both houses.

Congress has other important powers too. It controls the budget: it **collects taxes**, borrows money and **allocates funds** for federal programmes. It declares war, regulates trade between the states and with foreign countries, and issues money. The Senate approves the President's appointments and international treaties. The House of Representatives can impeach the President or other officials, and the Senate then decides whether to remove them from office. Congress also checks the work of the executive branch through hearings and investigations.

### 85. Describe the structure of the US Congress.

The US Congress is a **bicameral legislature**: it consists of the upper house, **the Senate**, and the lower house, **the House of Representatives**. Together they have 535 voting members.

The Senate has 100 senators — two from each state, whatever its size. Senators are elected for a six-year **term**, and every two years about one third of the Senate is re-elected. A senator must be at least 30 years old. The Vice President is the President of the Senate, but he can **vote** only to break a tie.

The House of Representatives has 435 members. The number of representatives from each state depends on its population, which is counted in a census every ten years: California has more than fifty, while some small states have only one. Representatives are elected for two years and must be at least 25. The House is led by the Speaker.

Both houses do most of their work in committees, where **bills** are discussed.

### 86. What are the powers of the state governments in the USA?

Each of the 50 US states has its own government with wide powers. According to the Tenth Amendment, all powers that the Constitution does not give to the federal government belong to the states or to the people.

Every state has its own constitution and three branches of power: a governor, who heads the executive branch, a state legislature, which **makes laws**, and state courts.

State governments are responsible for:
- public schools and state universities;
- the police, prisons and most criminal and civil laws;
- marriage, driving licences and traffic rules;
- organising **elections**, even federal ones;
- health care, local roads and local government.

States also **collect taxes** and **allocate funds** for their own programmes, so laws can be very different: for example, Texas and Florida have no state income tax, and only some states have the death penalty. However, states cannot print money, declare war or make treaties with foreign countries.

### 87. What is the purpose of the system of checks and balances? How does it work?

The system of checks and balances exists so that no branch of power can become too strong. Each branch — legislative, executive and judicial — can limit, or "check", the other two, so power stays balanced and people's freedoms are protected.

The idea comes from the French philosopher Montesquieu, and the authors of the US Constitution built it into the system in 1787. As James Madison wrote, "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition."

Here is how it works in the USA:
- the President can veto a **bill**, but Congress can override the veto by a two-thirds majority;
- the President **appoints** federal judges and Cabinet members, but **the Senate** must approve them;
- Congress controls the budget and can remove the President through impeachment;
- **the Supreme Court** can declare laws or the President's actions unconstitutional, and its judges serve for life, so they are independent.

So every branch needs the others, and none of them can rule alone.

### 88. Talk about the geographical location of Russia, its rivers and lakes.

Russia is the largest country in the world. It covers more than 17 million square kilometres in Eastern Europe and Northern Asia and stretches across 11 **time zones**. The Ural Mountains divide it into the European and the Asian parts.

Russia **borders on** more than a dozen countries, including Finland, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Mongolia and China. It **is washed by** twelve seas of three oceans — the Arctic, the Atlantic and the Pacific — and by the Caspian Sea, which is actually the largest lake on Earth.

There are more than two million rivers in Russia. The Volga, the longest river in Europe, flows into the Caspian Sea. The great Siberian rivers — the Ob, the Yenisei and the Lena — flow north into the Arctic Ocean, and the Amur flows into **the Pacific Ocean**. Lake Baikal is the deepest and oldest lake in the world and holds about a fifth of the world's fresh surface water. Lake Ladoga is the largest lake in Europe.

### 89. Talk about the climate and the diversity of landscapes in Russia.

Russia is so large that it has several climate zones and varied landscapes. Most of the country has a continental climate with long, cold winters and short, warm summers.

The climate becomes more continental from west to east. In the European part it is **temperate** and **milder**, while in Siberia winters are extremely cold: in Oymyakon, Yakutia, the temperature has fallen to about minus 68 degrees Celsius. The far north is Arctic, the Far East has a monsoon climate, and the Black Sea coast near Sochi is subtropical. More than half of the country lies on permafrost.

The **terrain** is mostly flat in the west: the East European **Plain** and the West Siberian Plain are among the largest in the world. In the south and east there are mountains: the Caucasus with Mount Elbrus, the Altai and the volcanoes of the Kamchatka **Peninsula**. From north to south, Arctic **deserts** and tundra change into taiga, mixed forests, steppes and semi-deserts.

### 90. What are the major sectors of Russia’s economy? Talk about each of them.

Russia has one of the largest economies in the world. It can be divided into three major sectors: industry, agriculture and services.

The fuel and energy sector, which includes oil, gas and coal **mining**, is the most important part of industry and brings a large part of export income. Other key industries are metallurgy, machine building, chemicals and fertilisers, and the nuclear, space and timber industries.

Agriculture uses huge areas of fertile **soil**, especially in the south. Russia is one of the world's leading grain producers and the largest exporter of wheat. Farmers also grow sunflowers and sugar beet and produce meat, milk and **poultry**.

The service sector is the largest by share of GDP. It includes trade, transport, banking, education, health care, tourism and IT. Transport is especially important for such a huge country: the Trans-Siberian Railway from Moscow to Vladivostok is the longest railway in the world.

The economy still depends a lot on exports of **raw materials**.

### 91. Talk about the agricultural sector of the economy in Russia.

Agriculture is an important sector of Russia's economy. The country has huge areas of farmland, and in recent years it has become one of the world's biggest food exporters, especially of grain.

The best farmland is in the southern regions: the Black Earth region, the Kuban, the Volga region and the south of Western Siberia. The black **soil**, called chernozem, is one of the most fertile soils in the world. In the north, however, the climate is too cold for most crops.

Russian agriculture has two main branches. Crop farming produces grain, first of all wheat, as well as sunflowers, sugar beet, potatoes and vegetables. Livestock farming gives beef, pork, **poultry**, eggs, milk and other **dairy products**. Fishing is also important, especially in the Far East, which is famous for salmon and red **caviar**.

Today Russia is the world's largest wheat exporter and a major exporter of sunflower oil.

### 92. What are the primary agricultural products in Russia, and which grains are the chief ones?

Russia's primary agricultural products are grain, sunflowers, sugar beet, potatoes and vegetables, as well as meat, milk and eggs. The chief grain is wheat, followed by barley, maize, rye and oats.

Wheat usually makes up about two-thirds of the grain harvest. It grows best on the fertile black **soil** of the south, and Russia is the world's largest wheat exporter. Barley is used for animal feed and for making beer, and rye is used for traditional Russian black bread. Buckwheat is also very popular in Russia, and the country is one of its main producers.

Sunflowers give sunflower oil, and sugar beet is used to make sugar. Potatoes are often called "the second bread" in Russia. Livestock farming provides beef, pork and **poultry**, as well as milk and **dairy products** like cheese, kefir and cottage cheese. In addition, Russia's seas and rivers give fish and **caviar**.

### 93. What are the key industries in Russia?

The key industries in Russia are the fuel and energy industry, metallurgy, machine building and the chemical industry. Many of them process the country's rich **raw materials**.

The oil and gas industry is the most important for exports. Most oil and gas is produced in Western Siberia and transported by pipelines and tankers. Coal **mining** is concentrated mainly in the Kuzbass.

Metallurgy is another leading industry. Big steel plants in Magnitogorsk, Cherepovets and Lipetsk use **iron ore** and coal. Aluminium, nickel and **copper** are produced too; aluminium plants in Siberia use cheap electricity from hydroelectric power stations.

Machine building produces cars and trucks, for example Lada in Tolyatti and KAMAZ in Naberezhnye Chelny, as well as aircraft, helicopters, ships and railway equipment. Russia builds nuclear-powered icebreakers, and it also builds nuclear power plants abroad. The chemical industry makes fertilisers and plastics. The space, defence, timber and food industries are also important.

### 94. What types of minerals and energy resources does Russia possess?

Russia is one of the richest countries in the world in natural resources, and almost every mineral can be found there. That's why **mining** plays a big role in its economy.

The most important are energy resources. Russia has the largest natural gas reserves in the world and is one of the biggest producers of **petroleum**. The main oil and gas fields are in Western Siberia, but there are also fields in the Volga–Ural region, on Sakhalin and in the Arctic. Russia is also rich in coal, especially in the Kuzbass, and has uranium and great hydroelectric resources.

There are also many metal ores. The Kursk Magnetic Anomaly is one of the largest **iron ore** deposits in the world, and Norilsk is famous for nickel, **copper** and palladium. Russia also produces gold, silver, **lead**, zinc and tin. Yakutia is famous for diamonds. Apatite from the Kola **Peninsula** and potassium salts from the Urals are used to make fertilisers.

### 95. What is the form of government in the Russian Federation?

According to its Constitution, the Russian Federation is a democratic federal state based on the rule of law, with a republican form of government. Political scientists usually describe it as a presidential, or more exactly, a semi-presidential (mixed) republic.

The Constitution was adopted by a nationwide **vote** on 12 December 1993 and amended in 2020. The head of state is the President, who is elected by the people for a six-year **term**. At the same time, there is a Government headed by the Prime Minister, whose candidacy must be approved by **the State Duma**. That is why the system is called mixed.

Russia is a federation: it **consists of** more than 80 constituent entities — republics, territories, regions and others. State power is divided into the legislative, executive and judicial branches. **The capital of** Russia is Moscow, and the state symbols are the white, blue and red flag, the coat of arms with a double-headed eagle and the national anthem.

### 96. Talk about the executive branch of power in Russia.

In Russia, executive power belongs to the Government of the Russian Federation. It works under the general direction of the President and is headed by the Chairman of the Government, usually called the Prime Minister. Since 2020 this post has been held by Mikhail Mishustin.

The Government consists of the Prime Minister, deputy prime ministers and federal ministers. The President proposes a candidate for Prime Minister, **the State Duma** approves him, and then the President **appoints** him. Most ministers are also approved by the Duma, but the heads of key ministries — Defence, Foreign Affairs, **the Ministry of Domestic Affairs** (Internal Affairs), **the Ministry of Justice** and some others — are appointed by the President after consultations with the Federation Council.

The Government runs the country day to day. It prepares the federal budget and **allocates funds** for the economy, science, education, health care and social security. It also manages federal property, ensures security, carries out foreign policy and fights crime.

### 97. What powers does the President of the Russian Federation possess?

The President of the Russian Federation is the head of state and the guarantor of the Constitution and of citizens' rights and freedoms. He is elected by the people for a six-year **term** and cannot hold the office for more than two terms. The current President, Vladimir Putin, was re-elected in 2024.

The President has wide powers:
- he determines the main directions of **domestic and foreign policy** and represents Russia abroad;
- he is the Supreme Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces;
- he **appoints** the Prime Minister with the approval of the State Duma and can dismiss the Government;
- he nominates judges of the Constitutional Court and **the Supreme Court**;
- he signs federal laws or can **reject** them;
- he calls elections to the State Duma and can dissolve it in some cases;
- he decides questions of **citizenship** and grants pardons.

The President also addresses the Federal Assembly with an annual message.

### 98. What is the primary role of the Federation Council, and what special powers does it have?

**The Federation Council** is the upper chamber of the Federal Assembly, the Russian parliament. Its primary role is to represent the interests of Russia's regions at the federal level.

Its members are called senators. Each region sends two senators: one from its legislative body and one from its executive body. In addition, the President can **appoint** up to 30 senators, and former Presidents can become senators for life.

The Federation Council approves or **rejects** laws passed by **the State Duma**. Laws on the budget, taxes, international treaties, borders, and war and peace must always be considered by it.

Its special powers include approving the President's decrees on martial law or a state of emergency and changes to the borders between regions. It decides whether the Armed Forces can be used outside Russia, calls presidential **elections** and can remove the President from office. On the President's proposal, it appoints judges of the Constitutional Court and **the Supreme Court**.

### 99. What do you know about the State Duma?

**The State Duma** is the lower chamber of the Federal Assembly, the Russian parliament. It is the main law-making body of the country.

The Duma has 450 deputies, who are elected for a five-year **term**. The **elections** are mixed: half of the deputies are elected from party lists, and the other half in single-member districts. Any Russian citizen aged 21 or older can be elected. The most recent elections took place in September 2026.

The main function of the Duma is to **make laws**. Every **bill** is first introduced in the State Duma; deputies discuss it and **vote**, and then the law goes to the Federation Council. The Duma also adopts the federal budget, approves the candidacy of the Prime Minister, can express no confidence in the Government, appoints the head of the Central Bank and announces amnesties.

The name "Duma" comes from the Russian verb "dumat" — "to think". The first State Duma appeared in 1906.

### 100. Talk about the three branches of power in Russia.

According to the Constitution, state power in Russia is divided into three branches: **legislative, executive and judicial**. They are independent of each other, and the President, as the head of state, ensures that they work together.

The legislative branch is the Federal Assembly, a **bicameral legislature**. Its upper chamber, **the Federation Council**, represents the regions, and its lower chamber, **the State Duma**, is elected by the people. The Federal Assembly makes federal laws, including the law on the budget.

The executive branch is the Government, headed by the Prime Minister. It carries out the laws, manages the economy and prepares the budget.

The judicial branch consists of the courts. The Constitutional Court checks whether laws agree with the Constitution, and **the Supreme Court** is the highest court for civil, criminal, administrative and economic cases. Judges are independent and obey only the Constitution and federal law.
